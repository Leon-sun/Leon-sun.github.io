A Wait Time Is Not a Property of a Patient

The question I was handed was: how should we stratify surgical cases? It is a reasonable question with a long institutional history behind it. Priority bands, target windows, a case is one thing or another and that determines what “on time” means for it.

After enough time with the data I came to think it is the wrong question — or at least one that cannot be answered on its own terms. A wait time is not a property of a patient. It is a property of a queue.

This is ongoing research at the University of Ottawa with CHEO, so what follows is the argument and the method. The numbers belong to the hospital and are not mine to publish.

What a priority band actually promises

A priority band encodes two things at once: a clinical judgment about urgency, and an institutional promise about a target window. The first is a statement about the patient. The second is a statement about the system.

Those come apart under load. Two cases in the same band, in the same specialty, referred in the same month, can wait very differently — not because anyone made a clinical error, but because they entered a system that was in different states on the two days they arrived. The band tells you what should happen. It has surprisingly little to say about what will.

So “are we hitting our targets by priority?” is a fair audit question and a bad design question. It measures the promise, not the mechanism that keeps or breaks it.

Two priorities, applied at two different moments

The first thing that complicated the stratification question was discovering there is more than one stratification.

A case carries a priority assigned at referral, when someone outside decides how urgently this patient needs to be seen. It carries another assigned at case request, when a surgeon decides how urgently this patient needs an operation. These are different judgments, made at different times, by different people, with different information — the second has the benefit of a consultation that had not happened yet when the first was made.

They do not map one-to-one, and they should not be expected to. A case referred as routine can become an urgent surgical request once someone has actually examined the patient. A case referred urgently can turn out to need an elective procedure, or none.

That is not a data quality problem. It is the system working correctly. But it means “stratification” is not one classification you can tune — it is at least two, and any wait-time target attached to a single band inherits the ambiguity between them. Before you can improve the stratification you have to say which one you mean, and at which moment the clock starts.

It is not a queue. It is two coupled queues.

The bigger problem is that the mental model underneath most wait-time analysis is a single line of people waiting for a single resource. That model is wrong here in four specific ways, and each one changes the answer.

It is multi-entry. Patients arrive by referral, by direct clinic visit, and by emergency. Emergency cases can go straight to the surgical queue, or be stabilized in clinic first and then generate a surgical case request like anyone else. There is no single front door to stand at and count.

It is not first-in-first-out. Service order is set by priority, not arrival. A case that arrives today can be operated on before one that has been waiting three months, correctly. Any intuition calibrated on FIFO queues — including most intuitions about what a backlog means — is unreliable here.

The queues are coupled. Clinic capacity gates surgical demand, because most surgical case requests are generated in clinic. And surgery feeds back into clinic, because post-operative follow-ups return and compete with new consultations for the same rooms and the same hours. Speeding up surgery increases follow-up demand on the clinic that was already the constraint on surgical requests.

There is feedback across time. What the system clears this week determines the backlog it starts next week with, which determines what it can clear. Wait time is not a static attribute you can average; it is the output of a process running against its own history.

One more thing that is easy to miss and matters a lot: the clinic serves patients who will never need surgery at all. They are not in the surgical dataset and they consume the same capacity. Model surgical flow in isolation and you will systematically overestimate how much room there is.

Why the descriptive view cannot answer the policy question

Everything above can be described from historical data, and describing it is genuinely useful — it is how you find out the mental model was wrong.

But the questions worth asking are counterfactual. If we moved one clinic session a week from follow-ups to new consultations, what would happen to the backlog? To the P1 patients? To the follow-up patients who lost the slot? No amount of descriptive statistics answers that, because the historical data contains exactly one policy: the one that was in force.

This is the same trap as measuring a search team on results-page conversion. The instrumentation can only report on the world as configured. To compare a configuration against one that has never existed, you need a model of the mechanism, not a summary of the outcome.

Two methods, one loop

The approach couples two techniques that are each insufficient alone.

Process mining supplies the pathway. From event logs you can reconstruct what actually happens to a case — the real sequence of clinic visits, requests, procedures and returns, including the rework loops nobody drew. This gives you transition probabilities grounded in observed behaviour rather than in a documented care pathway that describes intent.

Discrete-event simulation supplies the dynamics. Given arrivals by type and priority, given capacity, given the actual scheduling rules, you can run the queue forward and watch the backlog evolve. This gives you contention, which process mining has no concept of — a mined pathway tells you a case goes from A to B, not that it waited because eleven other cases wanted B first.

Neither works alone. Process mining is purely retrospective; it can tell you what the wait was and never what it would be. A queue simulation with invented pathways is a toy that reproduces your assumptions.

The loop matters more than either piece. Mined transition probabilities parameterize the simulation. Real events update the system state and re-estimate those probabilities, so the model tracks the hospital rather than drifting away from it. And critically, the simulation starts from the current backlog carried in as state, not from an empty system — a model that starts empty is answering a question nobody asked.

The decision it has to support

The output that earns its keep is not a forecast of next quarter's wait times. It is a comparison between policies you could actually adopt.

The concrete lever is the clinic template: how many slots per week go to new consultations, to pre-surgical follow-ups, to post-operative follow-ups. Reallocating that mix changes which queue absorbs the pressure.

The constraint that makes this useful is that total capacity is held fixed. A recommendation to hire more surgeons is easy to generate and impossible to act on this quarter. A recommendation to move two slots a week is a decision someone in the building can actually make.

And the trade-off has to be shown, not hidden. More new-visit capacity reduces backlog accumulation at the front of the system, at some cost to the people waiting for follow-ups. Whether that trade is acceptable is a clinical and ethical judgment, not a modelling one. The model's job is to put the exchange rate on the table so the people qualified to make that call can make it with their eyes open.

The honest limits

This is a prototype, not a deployed system, and I want to be precise about what is not yet established.

Event logs record what was recorded. Timestamps of documentation are not timestamps of care, and the gap is not random.

Simulated arrivals assume a distributional form. It will hold until something happens that breaks it, and outbreaks, staffing shocks and policy changes are exactly the conditions under which the model's answer would be most valuable.

Validation is the genuinely hard part. Reproducing history is necessary and nowhere near sufficient — a model can fit the past well and still be wrong about the counterfactual, which is the only thing you are using it for. I do not think that problem is solved here.

And no output of this becomes a scheduling change without clinical sign-off. The model makes a trade explicit; it does not have standing to accept it.

What generalizes

Check whether your unit of analysis is the case or the system. A great deal of operational analysis attributes to individual cases what is actually produced by contention between them. If the same case would get a different outcome depending on what else arrived that week, the case is not the unit.

Two classifications applied at different moments are two variables. Not one variable measured twice. Collapsing them loses precisely the information about how judgment changes as evidence arrives.

Hold capacity fixed and the recommendation becomes actionable. The valuable question is almost never “what would we do with more?” It is “what is the best arrangement of what we have?” The second is harder to model and far easier to adopt.

A model earns its place by making a trade-off explicit, not by producing a number. The number invites argument about the number. The trade-off invites a decision.

Requirements are negotiations, not transcripts

A stakeholder says: “we need a dashboard showing weekly volume by region.” You can write that down verbatim, build it, and deliver something nobody uses. I've done it. The sentence was accurate and the requirement was wrong.

The request was a solution someone had already designed in their head. The requirement was underneath it, unstated, and getting to it is a negotiation — not a transcription exercise.

The question that does the most work

[“What decision will this change?” If there's no answer, you've found something important. If there is one, you now know what the thing actually has to do — which is often much smaller than what was requested.]

Separating the ask from the need

  • The stated request — write it down verbatim, then set it aside rather than treating it as the spec.
  • The decision it's meant to support.
  • The constraint that made them ask now rather than six months ago.

How not to become the person who says no

[Argument: pushing back on a request without offering a better read of the need is just obstruction. The move is to come back with the underlying problem restated, and let them correct you — being corrected is the fastest form of elicitation there is.]

Write the acceptance criteria first

[Close: if you can't state how you'd know it worked, you haven't finished eliciting. Success criteria are cheap to argue about before development and expensive afterwards.]

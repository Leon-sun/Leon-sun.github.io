Knowing What a Customer Actually Cost

Part 2 of 4 on rebuilding paid growth at an automotive services platform.

For a long time, the team spending our entire acquisition budget could not see what a customer cost.

They were not careless. They were watching the only number available in real time, which happened to be the wrong one. That gap — between the number that is visible and the number that matters — turned out to be most of the work.

People buy tires once every couple of years, and the purchase completes in a physical store rather than on a screen. Paid acquisition was the gate to a relationship with no other way of starting. When acquisition is that central, knowing its true cost is not a reporting nicety.

Steering by the visible number

Ad platforms give you a real-time report. It tells you what an impression costs, what a click costs, how a campaign is pacing. It updates constantly, and it sits right where the operator is already working.

What it cannot tell you is whether a paying customer resulted. That lives in your own systems, days behind, in a different place — and in our case partly in a store where somebody physically fitted a tire.

So operations optimized against front-end cost, because front-end cost was what existed. Nobody chose this. It was the shape of the tools.

The consequence is easy to state and hard to notice from inside: a campaign with excellent cost per click can be your worst campaign. Cheap traffic is cheap for reasons, and those reasons show up two steps later in the funnel, where nobody was looking.

Attribution is a design decision, not a setting

Before you can compute a true cost per customer, you have to decide which spend gets credit for which customer. Most people treat this as configuration — pick last-click, move on. It is considerably less settled than that.

The platforms do not hand you comparable evidence. An image ad reports a click. A video ad on Douyin or Kuaishou reports a three-second view — which is barely intent at all, and conveniently generous to the party doing the reporting.

Under a naive last-touch rule those count as the same event. So a user who genuinely clicked an image ad on Monday, thought about it, and happened to scroll past three seconds of video on Thursday has the conversion credited to the video. At scale that shifts budget toward video for conversions clicks actually drove — and the data will look like video is working.

We stayed with last-touch, following local industry practice, with one correction: a real click was given a longer window to remain connected to a conversion than a three-second view was. Strong evidence of intent stays valid longer; weak evidence expires quickly and stops stealing credit.

That is not a clever trick. It is taking seriously that attribution encodes what you are willing to accept as evidence, and that every downstream number inherits your answer.

Everyone speaks a different language

The next obstacle was mundane and took months.

Every ad platform names things its own way — campaigns, ad groups, ads, placements — and defines its metrics on its own terms. What one calls a conversion, another calls an action. What one counts within a session, another counts within a day.

Which makes a question as basic as which channel gives us the cheapest new paying customer unanswerable — not for lack of data, but for lack of shared vocabulary. You cannot compare two things measured in different units by parties with different incentives.

So I read the platforms' API documentation, worked out what each field actually meant, and mapped everything into one internal language: a single set of report columns where a campaign is a campaign and a conversion is a conversion regardless of origin. Cost, exposure, clicks, front-end conversion, back-end conversion, ROI, new customers — plus the dimensions that make analysis possible at all, like which A/B variant and which landing page a user saw. Then I worked with the BI team to rebuild reporting on top of it.

This was not glamorous, and I initially resisted treating it as product work. It was the product work. Every comparison and every experiment we ran afterwards depends on that translation layer existing.

The chain that actually has to hold

Here is where the real difficulty sits.

To say this specific paying customer cost us this much, you have to connect: ad click → app user → placed order → paid → served. Five links, each a join across systems never designed to talk to each other, in a business where the last step happens in a physical store.

Every one of those joins is a decision, not a lookup. How long after a click does an app install still count as attributable? What if the user registered with a different phone number than they ordered with? What if they ordered on Tuesday and had the tires fitted on Saturday — which date owns the conversion?

Get one join wrong and you do not get an error message. You get a confidently wrong number that everyone then makes decisions against, which is worse than having no number at all.

Putting the real economics where people work

With the chain holding and a common language in place, we could finally build what operations had been missing: one view showing spend, whether a campaign had cleared its learning phase, the platform-reported cost, the real cost per new paying customer, and ROI.

The effect was simpler than I expected. Once true cost per customer was as visible and as current as cost per click, people stopped optimizing cost per click. Nobody had to be persuaded. The number they had been steering by was no longer the only one on the screen.

What we could and could not establish

Better measurement makes you more honest about what you know, not less.

Two things we could establish. Across channels, we could compare realized cost per new paying customer and ROI — which is exactly what the common language was built for, and how budget got allocated. And within a single channel, we could compare conversion goals against each other and see real differences, because audience, inventory and pricing are held roughly constant.

The distinction between those two is worth being careful about, because it is easy to blur. Comparing channels on realized cost tells you where to put the next dollar. It does not tell you why one channel is cheaper — if one channel is running mostly deep conversion goals and another mostly shallow ones, the difference between them says nothing about the goals, because everything else differs too. Allocation decisions can be made on cross-channel numbers. Strategy conclusions cannot, which is why the testing had to happen inside a channel.

In practice the two operate as nested levers, and the second matters most exactly where the first is constrained. Allocation is rarely free: commercial agreements commit you to a minimum spend on a platform whether or not it is currently your cheapest, so “move the money to the better channel” is available only within limits. When a channel's average cost runs high, you trim its budget as far as the commitments allow — and then you still have the mix inside it. A channel might be running most of its spend against payment-optimized goals and a minority against order-optimized ones; shifting that ratio changes what the platform's model goes looking for, and changes the realized cost, without moving a single dollar between channels.

So the same insight from the first post reappears one level down. The conversion event allocates budget whether or not anyone is managing it. Across channels you allocate with money. Within a channel, when the money is fixed, you allocate with the objective.

What we could not establish properly was incrementality. We ran well-powered comparisons across large groups of ads, deliberately at that grain because learning-phase variance makes any single campaign's numbers meaningless. But a comparison between two treated groups tells you which configuration is better, never whether treatment beat no treatment. What we did have was blunter: when spending stopped, traffic visibly fell off a cliff. That is worth something — it rules out the possibility that paid acquisition was purely harvesting demand that would have arrived anyway, which is the accusation always available against any paid channel. What it cannot tell you is the magnitude, or which parts of the spend were doing the work.

We never ran it properly for a reason worth naming, because it is the real reason most companies do not: a genuine holdout means deliberately not acquiring customers you could have acquired. The business would not accept that fluctuation, and that is not an unreasonable position when acquisition is how the company meets customers at all. The methodological answer is not to argue for the holdout — it is to make it cheaper. Geo holdouts suit a physical store network unusually well, because regional separation lets you buy the evidence with one market's fluctuation instead of the whole quarter's. That is what I would build first now.

What I would carry to another product

Measurement is a design problem, not a reporting one. Attribution windows, what counts as evidence, which join owns a conversion — these are product decisions with consequences, and they get made by default if nobody makes them deliberately.

People optimize what is visible in real time. Not what matters, not what they are told to care about — what is on the screen while they work. If the wrong number is the fast one, you will get the wrong behaviour from good people indefinitely. Fixing that beats another training session.

Check the vendor's number against your own. Ad platforms self-report conversions and are structurally motivated to attribute generously. Holding your own backend cost per paying customer as ground truth is not paranoia — it is the only version of the number with no interest in the answer.

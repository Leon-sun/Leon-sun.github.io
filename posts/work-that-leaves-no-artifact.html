The Work That Leaves No Artifact

Part 4 of 4 on rebuilding paid growth at an automotive services platform.

There is no feature called made three teams that did not respect each other build something together, even when that is the reason anything got built.

Most of what I did across two years of growth work does not appear in any release note. It also turned out to follow a single pattern, which I only recognised afterwards: when two groups could not work together, the answer was never to stand between them and translate. It was to build the shared object that made translating unnecessary.

People buy tires once every couple of years, and the purchase completes in a physical store. Paid acquisition — tens of millions of dollars a year across several platforms — was how the company met customers at all. Running it involved data science, ad operations, merchandising, engineering and BI, none of whom naturally spoke to each other.

Three teams, three views of the same problem

Data science thought the operations team did not understand ad technology. The ad team thought data science did not understand operational reality. Engineering built exactly what was specified and nothing beyond it, because that is what engineering is asked to do when nobody has decided what the thing should be.

Each read was partly accurate, which is the least useful kind of disagreement. Everyone came to me, and for a while I did the obvious thing: I relayed. I explained operations to data science and data science to operations, translated business intent into specifications for engineering, and carried the answers back.

It worked, and it did not scale, and it made me the single point of failure for every decision. Being the translator is a good way to become indispensable and a bad way to be useful.

The creative loop

The clearest case was between the ad team and merchandising.

Ad creative was designed by one group and the products and promotions behind it by another, and the two argued in the way people argue when they have no shared evidence — from position, repeatedly. Merchandising believed certain products deserved better promotion. The ad team believed certain creatives worked. Neither could show the other anything.

The reason was structural. Creative performance data dies inside the ad platform. You learn which creative got clicks and nothing after that, because the platform has no idea what happened once the user reached your app.

So we standardized creative metadata across business units and carried it into our own analytics, until the internal user funnel could show which creative had brought each user in — and then what that user actually did. Which products they looked at. Whether they bought. What they were worth.

That single connection changed the conversation into a different kind of conversation. The ad team could see where creative promise and merchandising reality diverged. Merchandising could read the same data, improve their designs, and send new promotion ideas back for the ad team to build campaigns from. It ran both directions, and neither side needed me in the middle, because they now had a shared artifact to point at.

It also surfaced something embarrassing that nobody had been positioned to notice. Different ads promised different promotions, and every one of them dropped the user onto a generic app home screen after download. The promise made in the ad did not survive the arrival. We made the promotion continue into the app — tested, clearly positive, rolled out everywhere. That fix was worth real money and it had been invisible for years, not because it was subtle but because no single team could see both ends of it.

Bricks, not detours

Doing this kind of work requires surviving the constant pressure of urgent requests, and I found only one approach that does not eventually fail.

The two obvious options both lose. Refuse tactical work to protect the roadmap, and you lose the trust of the people asking — they stop bringing you problems, which means you stop hearing about the real ones. Accept everything and build it as throwaway, and you accrete debt and never converge on anything.

What I settled into was building the urgent thing as a brick — a component of the architecture I was heading toward — while continuing to explain the architecture out loud. The cross-platform reporting layer, the per-campaign configuration mechanism and the recommendation layer all began as something somebody needed that week. Each ended up as a piece of the platform.

This is less about discipline than about sequencing. Almost any urgent request can be built in a version that is also a step toward where you are going, if you know where that is and you are willing to spend a little longer on it.

Learning to stop being the answer

The same principle applied to people, and I was slower to see it there.

I had junior product managers and new graduates working on this, and for a long time I was still operating as an individual contributor. The pace was relentless and it was faster to deliver the solution myself. I told myself I was unblocking them.

What I eventually understood is that neither micromanaging nor teaching transfers ownership. Being available is what does the damage. As long as a stakeholder can route around a junior PM and get an answer from me, that PM owns nothing — they are executing my decisions with extra steps, and everyone including them knows it.

So I gave them whole modules and then refused to take any requirement inside them. If someone came to me about their module, they went to that person instead.

It was not reckless. I started them on the least risky pieces and widened as they proved they could hold it, and a weekly review meant nothing large could go wrong unnoticed. The failures that happened were revisions and delays, not damage — the cost I had decided was worth paying.

The uncomfortable part was the middle. They made mistakes. People kept coming to me anyway. Holding the line while both of those were true, for about two months, was the whole of the work. After that they operated independently, their manager reviews came back solid, and one of them was promoted after a poor review the cycle before.

The pattern

I did not plan any of this as a method. Looking back, the same move appears each time.

The ad team and merchandising did not need a translator; they needed a shared artifact — creative connected to real customer behaviour — that let them argue productively without me. Operations and data science did not need better explanations; they needed the models to arrive somewhere they were already looking. The junior PMs did not need more of my time; they needed stakeholders to have nowhere else to go.

In each case the instinct is to insert yourself, because inserting yourself works immediately and feels like leadership. What actually works is building the thing that makes you unnecessary for that particular problem, and then not being available.

Which is why this is the hardest part of the job to describe in an interview. When it goes well, the evidence is your own absence from the loop.
